How Hermes is made: the published recipe

The training recipe behind the model you have been running - all published, all reproducible in spirit. DataForge synthesizes the data, Atropos rejects the bad samples, and 192 GPUs bake it in. Every fact on this page traces to the Hermes 4 technical report.

Why read this

Using a tool is level 1. Knowing how it was made is level 2 - and with Hermes, level 2 is actually open to you. Most labs publish a glossy blog post and hide the recipe; Nous published a 41-page technical report (arXiv 2508.18255) that walks through the data factory, the quality filter, and the training run in enough detail to reproduce the approach. This page turns that report into a guided tour. Nothing here is speculation - every number cites a section.

Part 1 · covers the report's data pipeline, §2

DataForge - the data factory ~9 min read

Hermes 4 was post-trained on roughly 5 million samples, about 19 billion tokens (4.3 scaled to ~60B) - and most of it was newly synthesized, not scraped. The machine that made it is called DataForge, and it is the most original idea in the report.

Self-studyWhy synthetic data won3 min read▶

The obvious way to post-train a model is to collect human conversations and imitate them. The Hermes team went the other way: the report states the corpus is mostly newly synthesized - generated by models, filtered by machines, at a scale and precision no human labeling budget can match. Three reasons this won:

  • Precision of behavior. You cannot scrape "always emit valid JSON matching this Pydantic schema" from the web. You CAN generate a million examples of exactly that behavior and verify each one programmatically.
  • Coverage on demand. Human data clusters around what humans happen to write about. A generator can be pointed at the gaps - rare formats, unusual personas, adversarial phrasings - deliberately.
  • Verifiability. Synthetic data can be born with a checkable answer key. That is what makes Part 2's rejection sampling possible: you can only reject bad samples if you can define "bad" mechanically.

The honest tradeoff: synthetic data inherits the quirks of whatever generated it, and naive generation collapses into repetitive sameness. The whole design of DataForge - and the covering-sets idea two cards down - exists to fight that sameness.

Real world

The lineage. The report credits Microsoft's AgentInstruct as inspiration: the idea that agentic pipelines of models - generating, transforming, critiquing - can manufacture better instruction data than humans can label. DataForge is that idea rebuilt as a formal graph.

Self-studyThe DAG idea - PDDL-style nodes and random walks4 min read▶

DataForge (report §2) is a directed acyclic graph where every node is one transformation with a formal contract, in the style of PDDL - the classical planning language:

  • Each node is a struct-to-struct transformation. It takes a typed input (say, a raw web article), does one job (turn it into a lecture transcript, translate it, derive a quiz from it), and emits a typed output.
  • Preconditions and postconditions. A node declares what must be true of its input before it runs and what it guarantees about its output. That is what makes nodes safely composable - the same trick classical planners use to chain actions.
  • Random walks generate diversity. A training sample is made by walking a random valid path through the graph. Different paths = different chains of transformations = structurally different conversations, from the same seed material.
  • Graphs compose. A whole graph can become a node in a higher-order graph. Small verified pipelines snap together into bigger ones without anyone re-checking the plumbing.

The punchline: diversity is not left to a prompt that says "please be diverse". It is a structural property of how many distinct paths exist through the graph.

Self-studyCovering sets - taxonomies + PersonaHub2 min read▶

Random walks give structural diversity; covering sets give topical and human diversity. The report describes seeding generation against explicit coverage targets:

  • Taxonomies - enumerated trees of subjects, task types, and difficulty levels. Generation is steered so the corpus covers the tree instead of clustering on whatever a generator finds easy.
  • PersonaHub - a public library of hundreds of thousands of synthetic personas. Asking the same question as a retired marine biologist, a teenage speedrunner, and a tax auditor yields genuinely different conversations - tone, vocabulary, and what "a good answer" even means.

This is the same discipline a survey designer uses: define the population you need to cover FIRST, then sample against it. It is also why Hermes holds personas so well in Session 3 - it was trained on data that took personas seriously.

Part 2 · covers the report's rejection sampling, §2

Rejection sampling - only the good survives ~7 min read

Generating candidate data is cheap; the value is in what you throw away. Hermes 4 is a pure SFT recipe - no RLHF headline stage - so quality control happens BEFORE training: candidate generations are run through automated verifier environments, and only samples that pass get to become training data.

Self-studyThe Atropos environments - what "good" means, mechanically4 min read▶

The verifiers run inside Atropos, Nous's open framework of RL environments (github.com/NousResearch/atropos) - here used not for RL, but as a battery of pass/fail judges for rejection sampling. The report describes environments including:

EnvironmentWhat it enforces
Answer formatRoughly 1,000 distinct output formats, checked strictly - including exact <think></think> delimiter usage. This is why the reasoning tags you met in Session 4 are so reliable.
Instruction followingDid the response actually obey the constraints in the prompt - length, structure, forbidden words, ordering?
InternbootcampA large suite of verifiable reasoning tasks with checkable answers.
Schema adherenceJSON outputs validated against Pydantic schemas - the ancestry of Session 5's "JSON that validates" behavior.
Tool useTool calls checked for well-formed structure and correct invocation - the ancestry of Session 6.

Notice the pattern: every behavior you have relied on in this course - clean think tags, valid JSON, disciplined tool calls - maps to a specific verifier that filtered the training data. Reliability was not hoped for; it was selected for.

Why this beats "train on everything" A wrong sample does not just waste tokens - it actively teaches the wrong behavior. Rejection sampling means the model only ever imitates demonstrations that provably passed. The filter IS the alignment mechanism in a pure-SFT recipe.
Self-studyThe honest note: Atropos is archived2 min read▶

Full transparency, because this course does not paper over inconvenient facts: the Atropos repository was archived (made read-only) on July 4, 2026. You can still read every line of it, but it is no longer maintained.

  • How to teach it, then: as history - "this is how Hermes 4 was built" - not as a live tool to adopt for your own projects today.
  • Why it still matters: the design pattern (a library of programmatic pass/fail environments doing double duty for RL and for data filtering) outlives any one repo. If you ever build a data pipeline, this is the pattern to steal.
  • The code is still evidence. An archived repo is a frozen, checkable record of exactly how the published recipe worked. Few labs leave that much on the table for you.
Part 3 · covers the report's training setup, §3 + §3.1

The training run ~9 min read

Data made, data filtered - now bake it in. The run itself is disciplined engineering rather than magic, and the report publishes every number. Then comes the cleverest section in the whole paper: teaching the model to stop thinking.

Hold the scale in perspective: 192 GPUs is a serious cluster, but it is post-training, not pretraining - orders of magnitude below what it took to make the base models. The leverage is in the data recipe, which is exactly why Parts 1 and 2 came first.

Self-studyTwo quiet tricks: loss masking and bin packing3 min read▶

Two unglamorous details in §3 do a lot of work:

  • Loss on assistant tokens only. Training conversations contain system prompts, user turns, and assistant replies - but the loss is computed only on the assistant's tokens. The model learns to WRITE good answers, not to imitate users or parrot system prompts. This is standard practice done carefully, and it matters for steerability: system prompts stay instructions, not content to reproduce.
  • First-Fit-Decreasing packing. Samples have wildly different lengths; padding short ones to 16,384 tokens would burn most of the cluster on nothing. FFD - the classic bin-packing heuristic - packs samples into sequences so tightly that the report claims over 99.9% batch efficiency. Almost no GPU-hour is spent computing on padding.
Real world

The suitcase analogy. FFD is how an experienced packer fills a suitcase: biggest items first, then progressively smaller ones into the remaining gaps. Same algorithm, applied to token sequences, worth real money at 192-GPU scale.

Self-studyTeaching it to stop thinking - the second SFT stage4 min read▶

Report §3.1 tells the best story in the paper. Reasoning models have a failure mode: they think too long. Hermes 4 14B, with its 40,960-token limit, hit that cap on 60% of LiveCodeBench reasoning runs - burning the whole budget mid-thought and truncating.

The fix is elegant: a second SFT stage that teaches the model to wrap up - to close its </think> tag by around 30,000 tokens and commit to an answer. No new knowledge, just a learned sense of when to stop deliberating.

  • The cost is real and published: in that condition, Hermes 4 405B drops from 55.0 to 52.4 on AIME'24 - a few points of peak reasoning, traded away deliberately.
  • The win is bigger: overlong generations collapse. In production, a model that reliably finishes inside its budget beats one that occasionally thinks brilliantly and often runs off a cliff.
  • The takeaway for you: this is why your local Hermes self-terminates its reasoning around ~30k tokens (the Session 4 behavior). It was explicitly trained to.

And the payoff at eval time - Hermes 4 405B in reasoning mode: MATH-500 96.3, AIME'24 81.9, GPQA Diamond 70.5, LiveCodeBench v6 61.3, MMLU 87.2, Arena-Hard v1 94.4, and RefusalBench 57.1 (deep dive D5 teaches you to read that last one critically).

Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 30-45 min total

  • Read ONE section of the actual report: open arXiv 2508.18255 and pick §2 (the data factory) or §3.1 (reasoning-length control). Primary sources are less scary than they look, and this one is well written.
  • Run a format-adherence probe on your local Hermes: ask it 10 times for the same answer in a strict format (for example: "Answer in exactly 3 bullets, each under 10 words, no preamble"). Count how many of 10 comply perfectly. You are reproducing, in miniature, what the answer-format environment measured.
  • Now try the same probe on any other model you use and compare the compliance rate. The gap (or lack of one) is Part 2 made visible.
  • Optional: skim the archived Atropos repo (github.com/NousResearch/atropos) and find the answer-format environment. Reading real verifier code beats any summary of it.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · How does DataForge generate diverse training data?

Diversity is structural, not requested: different random paths through the graph chain different transformations, and covering sets (taxonomies + PersonaHub) steer topical and persona coverage on top.

2 · In the Hermes 4 recipe, what role does Atropos play?

Hermes 4 is pure SFT - no RLHF headline stage. Quality control happens before training: the Atropos verifiers (answer format with ~1k formats, instruction following, Internbootcamp, Pydantic schemas, tool use) reject bad candidates so the model only imitates verified-good demonstrations.

3 · What did the second SFT stage (report §3.1) teach the model, and at what cost?

The 14B was hitting its 40,960-token cap on 60% of LiveCodeBench reasoning runs. The second stage teaches it WHEN to stop - trading a little peak reasoning for a model that reliably finishes inside its budget.
